## What this is about

**MDI** —the Inference Infrastructure Sizing Model for mixed human and agent
loads— answers two symmetric questions: given a load, how much hardware it
takes; and given the hardware, how much load it covers.

Serving a language model is not a "how much GPU do I need" problem but a matter
of three competing budgets: how many bytes fit in VRAM, how many bytes can be
moved within the time the user tolerates, and how many multiplications fit in
that same time. All three are evaluated on the same GPU, and the answer is
always the most restrictive of the three.

The **mixed loads** part is not decoration. A human and an agent are nowhere
near the same unit of demand: the agent generates almost all the time and drags
a context ten times larger. Treating them as the same thing is the mistake that
leaves a sizing exercise short by an order of magnitude, and much of this
document exists to put a number on that difference.

The equations are derived here, and at the end sits the calculator that applies
them. All the math runs in your browser: no server, nothing is sent anywhere,
and the configuration you build travels in the URL so you can share it as a
link.

## Before the loop: prefill

Every request goes through two phases that behave in opposite ways, and it pays
to separate them before getting into the equations.

The first is **prefill**: the model processes the entire prompt before emitting
anything. All the input tokens go through in parallel, in a single matrix
operation, and what comes out is not text but the **KV cache** that the second
phase will consult at every step. For each prompt token, two vectors are
computed and stored in every attention layer —a key, which says how that token
announces itself to the ones that come after, and a value, which is the
information it contributes when something attends to it—. That is where the
factors of the $\mathrm{KV}_t$ that appears in everything below come from: the
2 is key and value, and the rest is how many layers and of what size.

Prefill is a compute problem, not a memory one: it processes thousands of
tokens at once and saturates the Tensor Cores. Its metric is **TTFT** —*time to
first token*, the time the user spends staring at an empty screen— and it
scales with the length of the prompt:

$$
\mathrm{TTFT} \;=\; \frac{2 \cdot N \cdot 10^{9} \cdot T_{\text{in}}}{F}
$$

where $T_{\text{in}}$ is the number of prompt tokens and $F$ the effective
FLOPS, the same ones the efficiency factor has already discounted.

Decode is the opposite. It generates one token at a time, with nothing to
parallelize, and at every step it reads the entire model from memory again. It
is a bandwidth problem, and it is where nearly all the time in a response goes:
a thousand-token prompt is processed in tens of milliseconds, while generating
a thousand tokens takes several seconds.

**The rest of this document is about decode**, because it is the phase that
pins down the hardware. Prefill comes back later, in the limitations, where it
competes for the same GPU that is decoding.

## The decode loop

An autoregressive model generates one token at a time. To produce the next one,
the GPU has to do two things: **read** the model weights from memory and
**multiply** the activation by them. Both happen at once, in different units of
the chip, so the step takes as long as the slower of the two.

The metric the user cares about is **TPOT** — *time per output token*, the gap
between two consecutive tokens on screen. A TPOT of 30 ms is about 33 tokens
per second, roughly twice as fast as comfortable reading.

<DiagramaCiclo />

In a decode step with batch $B$ and average context $C$ per sequence, the
memory traffic is:

$$
\text{bytes per step} \;=\; \underbrace{P_m}_{\text{weights}} \;+\; \underbrace{B \cdot C \cdot \mathrm{KV}_t}_{\text{cache}}
$$

and the arithmetic work, counting two operations — one multiply and one add —
per parameter and per sequence:

$$
\text{FLOP per step} \;=\; 2 \cdot N \cdot 10^{9} \cdot B
$$

where **$N$ is in billions of parameters** — the convention of the code and of
the catalogs, where Qwen3.5-27B is `N = 27`, not `N = 27000000000`. That is why
the $10^{9}$ factor appears explicitly in **every** compute equation from here
on: without it they are not dimensionally correct.

Dividing each one by the resource it consumes:

$$
\mathrm{TPOT} \;=\; \max\!\left( \frac{P_m + B \cdot C \cdot \mathrm{KV}_t}{W},\; \frac{2 N 10^{9} B}{F} \right)
$$

where $W$ is the effective bandwidth in bytes/s and $F$ the effective FLOPS. It
is a maximum, not a sum: the two paths advance in parallel.

### The theoretical floor

If the batch is a single sequence and the context is negligible, the cache term
vanishes and what remains is the most useful number in the whole analysis:

$$
\mathrm{TPOT}_{\min} \;=\; \frac{P_m}{W}
$$

A 27-billion-parameter model in fp8 weighs 27 GB. On an H100 SXM with 3350 GB/s
nominal and an efficiency factor of 0.5 — that is, 1675 GB/s in practice — the
floor is around 16 ms per token. **No configuration, no batch, no serving
optimization is going below that without changing the model, the quantization,
or the hardware.** Everything else in this document is how much that floor
degrades as load is added.

## What fills the VRAM

Two big things and one medium-sized one.

**The weights.** The entire model has to be resident. Its size depends on the
quantization, $b_w$ bytes per parameter:

$$
P_m \;=\; N \cdot 10^{9} \cdot b_w
$$

**The KV cache.** For every token that has already gone through the model, two
vectors — key and value — have to be stored in each layer that does attention.
The cost per token of context is:

$$
\mathrm{KV}_t \;=\; 2 \cdot L_a \cdot H \cdot d_k \cdot b_{kv}
$$

The factor of 2 is key and value. $L_a$ is **the layers that generate cache,
not the total**: hybrid architectures interleave attention layers with layers
that are not, and that is where almost all the difference between two models of
the same size lives. $H$ is the KV heads, which with *grouped-query attention*
are far fewer than the query heads, and $d_k$ the dimension per head.

The difference is brutal. A dense 27B with 46 attention layers and GQA 8×128
spends 92 KB per token of context in fp8. A hybrid 27B with 16 attention layers
and 4 heads of 256 spends 32 KB in fp8 — almost three times less. At 30,000
tokens of context that is the difference between 2.6 GB and 0.9 GB **per
session**.

**Engine overhead.** Activation buffers, CUDA graphs, the scheduler,
fragmentation in the block allocator. A couple of gigabytes you never get back;
in the equations it shows up as $O$.

What is left over is the only thing that can go to the cache:

$$
\mathrm{KV}_{\max}^{\text{mem}} \;=\; V_t - P_m - O
$$

## The three constraints

This is where the problem gets interesting. There are three ceilings and they
act on different things.

<DiagramaTechos />

**Memory.** The cache bytes that physically fit, with the weights and the
overhead already subtracted:

$$
\mathrm{KV}_{\max}^{\text{mem}} \;=\; V_t - P_m - O
$$

If this comes out negative, the model does not fit on that GPU and there is
nothing to discuss.

**Latency.** The SLO bounds how many bytes can be read per step. The weights
have already eaten $P_m$ of that budget; what is left goes to the cache:

$$
\mathrm{KV}_{\max}^{\text{lat}} \;=\; \mathrm{SLO} \cdot W - P_m
$$

If this comes out negative, the SLO is unreachable on that GPU even with VRAM
to spare: reading the weights alone no longer fits in the time asked for.

**Compute.** The third ceiling does not bound bytes but **sequences**. The
largest batch the Tensor Cores sustain within the SLO is:

$$
B_{\text{comp}} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{SLO} \cdot F}{2 N \cdot 10^{9}}
$$

Note that the first two ceilings are measured in cache bytes and the third in
number of sequences. That is why they cannot be added or averaged: each one is
compared against demand in its own unit, and only then is the worst taken.

## The batch and why it comes almost free

The weights are read **once per step**, no matter how many sequences are in the
batch. The cache, on the other hand, is paid per sequence.

<DiagramaLote />

Per useful token generated, the cost is:

$$
\frac{\text{bytes}}{\text{token}} \;=\; \frac{P_m}{B} \;+\; C \cdot \mathrm{KV}_t
$$

The first term dilutes with the batch; the second does not. With short contexts
the system operates in the zone where the weights dominate and adding sessions
is almost free: that is the entire economics of served inference. With long
contexts the second term rules and every new session costs full price.

That is why context is the variable that moves the result most, more than the
number of users. Doubling the users doubles the cache demand; doubling the
context does too, but it also pushes the system toward the regime where the
batch no longer helps.

## How much load there really is

A registered user consumes nothing while reading. What counts is how many
sessions are **generating** at any given instant. Each population is described
by three numbers: how many there are ($U$), what fraction of the time they
generate ($D$, the *duty cycle*), and how much context they drag along ($C$).

$$
A \;=\; U \cdot D
$$

With two populations — humans and agents — the total cache demand is:

$$
\mathrm{KV}_{\text{total}} \;=\; \mathrm{KV}_t \cdot \left( U_h D_h C_h + U_a D_a C_a \right)
$$

The difference between the two populations is not one of degree but of kind. A
human types, waits, reads, gets distracted: generating maybe 15% of the time,
with a few thousand tokens of context. An agent chains tool calls with no human
pauses and drags along the entire conversation plus the results of every tool:
it can be generating 95% of the time carrying tens of thousands of tokens.

### κ — what an agent costs in users

The ratio between what an agent costs and what a user costs is:

$$
\kappa \;=\; \frac{D_a \cdot C_a}{D_h \cdot C_h}
$$

With the default values — 15% and 3,000 tokens against 95% and 30,000 — a
single agent is worth **63 users**. That is the number to keep in your head
when planning: forty coding agents weigh more than two thousand people using a
chat.

$\kappa$ is also the slope of the trade-off frontier in capacity mode: every
agent added to the plan evicts $\kappa$ users.

## Sizing mode: how many GPUs it takes

Given the load, each constraint independently demands a minimum number of GPUs.
The first two divide up cache bytes; the third divides up sequences.

$$
G_{\text{mem}} = \frac{\mathrm{KV}_{\text{total}}}{V_t - P_m - O}
\qquad
G_{\text{lat}} = \frac{\mathrm{KV}_{\text{total}}}{\mathrm{SLO} \cdot W - P_m}
\qquad
G_{\text{comp}} = \frac{A}{B_{\text{comp}}}
$$

The result is the worst of the three, rounded up and with a floor of one unit:

$$
G \;=\; \max\!\left(1,\; \left\lceil \max\left(G_{\text{mem}},\, G_{\text{lat}},\, G_{\text{comp}}\right) \right\rceil \right)
$$

Reporting **which of the three dominates** matters as much as the number,
because it determines what to do about it. If memory rules, you want more VRAM
or less context. If latency rules, you want more bandwidth — and adding VRAM
does nothing at all. If compute rules, the batch has saturated the Tensor Cores
and only a smaller model or more units helps.

With $G$ decided, the actual batch per GPU is $B = A / G$ and the TPOT you
actually get comes out of the same equation as at the start:

$$
\mathrm{TPOT} \;=\; \max\!\left( \frac{P_m + \mathrm{KV}_{\text{total}} / G}{W},\; \frac{2 N 10^{9} B}{F} \right)
$$

That TPOT usually lands **below** the SLO, because rounding up hands you
headroom. That headroom is real and can be spent on context, on users, or on
sleeping at night.

## Capacity mode: how much load it covers

The inverse question is more common than it seems: the hardware is already
bought and what you want to know is how many people you can promise service to.

You fix $G$ units and a number of agents, and solve for how many humans fit on
top. The available byte ceiling is the lower of the two byte ceilings,
multiplied by the units:

$$
\mathrm{KV}_{\text{avail}} \;=\; G \cdot \min\!\left( \mathrm{KV}_{\max}^{\text{mem}},\; \mathrm{KV}_{\max}^{\text{lat}} \right)
$$

Subtracting what the agents already took and dividing by what one registered
user costs:

$$
U_h \;\le\; \frac{\mathrm{KV}_{\text{avail}} - U_a \cdot \mathrm{KV}_t D_a C_a}{\mathrm{KV}_t D_h C_h}
$$

And in parallel, the compute constraint on that same split:

$$
U_h \;\le\; \frac{G \cdot B_{\text{comp}} - U_a D_a}{D_h}
$$

The result is the smaller of the two. If the agents alone already do not fit,
there are no users to add and the configuration simply is not enough.

The two modes share the same core: they are the same inequality solved for
different unknowns. If sizing returns $G$ for a load, capacity with that same
$G$ has to admit that load — and the engine's tests check exactly that across
the whole catalog.

## Regimes and crossovers

Which constraint rules is not a property of the hardware: **it depends on the
context**. With short contexts the cache is negligible and the system is a
compute problem. As the context grows, the cache starts to dominate the traffic
and the bottleneck migrates toward latency, and then toward memory.

<DiagramaRegimenes />

Careful how you read that: **there are two regimes, not three**. Which of the
two byte ceilings rules —memory or latency— does not depend on the context at
all, so the system moves from compute to whichever byte ceiling is tighter and
stays there. The two crossovers below are the same jump measured against each
ceiling; the one that happens first is the real one, and the other sits behind
it changing nothing.

The crossovers come from setting the constraints equal in pairs. Compute
against latency:

$$
C_{\text{eq}1} \;=\; \frac{\left(\mathrm{SLO} \cdot W - P_m\right) \cdot 2 N \cdot 10^{9}}{\mathrm{SLO} \cdot F \cdot \mathrm{KV}_t}
$$

And compute against memory:

$$
C_{\text{eq}3} \;=\; \frac{\left(V_t - P_m - O\right) \cdot 2 N \cdot 10^{9}}{\mathrm{SLO} \cdot F \cdot \mathrm{KV}_t}
$$

The $10^{9}$ is the same one as always: $N$ is in billions of parameters, so
every time parameters are turned into FLOP that scale has to be undone. Without
it the crossovers land on the order of a millionth of a token instead of the
~1,300 the calculator gives, which is a good way to check it is in the right
place.

The crossover between memory and latency is direct, because both ceilings bound
bytes: memory rules when $V_t - P_m - O \le \mathrm{SLO} \cdot W - P_m$, that
is, when the VRAM runs out before the time budget does. That regime does not
depend on the context at all — it is a property of the GPU against the SLO.

There are also two absolute ceilings that no amount of added sessions can beat:

$$
\left.\frac{\text{tok}}{\text{s}}\right|_{\text{memory}} = \frac{W}{\mathrm{KV}_t \cdot C}
\qquad
\left.\frac{\text{tok}}{\text{s}}\right|_{\text{compute}} = \frac{F}{2 N \cdot 10^{9}}
$$

The first falls linearly with the context. The second does not depend on the
context at all. Where they cross is another way of seeing where the boundary
between regimes sits.

## The efficiency factor

Everything above comes out optimistic, and by considerably more than a margin
of error. The reasons are well known and none of them is a flaw in the model:

- The cache competes for the same bandwidth as the weights, and the access is
  not perfectly sequential.
- There is kernel-launch overhead between layers.
- The inference engine's *scheduler* does not keep the batch full all the time:
  sequences come and go.
- The vendor's nominal bandwidth is measured under conditions that a real
  inference engine does not reproduce.

Rather than model each effect separately, the engine folds them into a single
factor $\eta$ that discounts **both the bandwidth and the FLOPS**:

$$
W = \eta \cdot W_{\text{nominal}}
\qquad
F = \eta \cdot F_{\text{nominal}}
$$

The default, $\eta = 0.5$, is what public benchmarks on large models back up:
half of nominal, that is, a real TPOT on the order of twice the theoretical
one. It is a parameter, not a physical constant: **if you have a real
measurement from your inference engine, use it**. It is the knob that changes
the result most after context.

## How to read the calculator

In **sizing** mode each point on the chart is a GPU: on the horizontal axis the
TPOT it achieves, on the vertical what the number of units required costs per
hour. The band on the right is the zone that violates the SLO. The dotted line
joins the Pareto front — off it there is always a better option in both
dimensions at once.

The pressure bars in the table show how many GPUs each constraint demands on
its own. The filled bar is the one that sets the total; the other two, dimmed,
are the headroom left over.

In **capacity** mode each curve is a GPU. Every point on it saturates the
system, so there is no single optimum: it is the real trade-off between serving
agents and serving users, and the slope is $\kappa$. The elbow that shows up in
some curves is the point where the bottleneck switches from bytes to compute.

The colors are consistent across the site: **pine green** when memory rules,
**burnt orange** when latency rules, **blue** when compute rules.

## Limitations

This model is a first-order bound, useful for deciding the order of magnitude
of the hardware and for understanding which constraint rules. It is not a
simulator. Here is what it does **not** account for, in rough order of impact:

**Prefix caching.** The calculation assumes every session has its own complete
cache. In practice, agents that share a system prompt —or the same repository
loaded into context— can reuse the prefix across sessions, and the saving is
enormous: what gets shared is exactly the $C_a$ term, and that is the dominant
term. **With prefix caching on, this calculation oversizes**, sometimes by a
considerable factor.

**MoE models.** The compute formula and the weight-traffic formula both assume
that **all** the parameters are read and used at every token. In a mixture of
experts only a fraction activates, so $N$ ought to be the active parameter
count for compute and the total for VRAM: two different numbers, and here there
is only one. For an MoE, this model does not apply without modifying it.

**Tensor parallelism.** A full replica of the model per GPU is assumed. With
tensor parallelism the weights are split across several units, which changes
both $P_m$ per GPU and the traffic pattern —and adds inter-GPU communication,
which appears nowhere here.

**Demand variability.** The whole calculation uses averages: average duty
cycle, average context. Real demand arrives in bursts, and a queue sized to the
average saturates at the peak. **Peaks require headroom**, and how much is a
decision this model does not make for you.

**Chunked prefill and the effect of prefill on decode.** Processing the initial
prompt is a compute-bound operation, not a memory-bound one, and it competes
for the same GPU that is decoding. Modern engines interleave chunks of prefill
between decode steps, which degrades the TPOT of the active sessions in a way
that is not modeled here at all.

On top of that, two warnings about the default values:

- **The duty cycles are pinned to the worst case** and are deliberately not
  editable: 0.15 for humans and 0.95 for agents. Real traces from coding agents
  look more like ~0.42 —thirteen seconds generating for every eighteen in
  tools—; 0.95 corresponds to an agent with very fast tools. If your agents
  look more like the first figure, the sizing comes out generous.
- **The prices are indicative.** They are meant to represent amortized CAPEX
  plus OPEX, they vary wildly across providers and regions, and they are
  editable in the table precisely because there is no correct number. Do not
  use them as a quote.

All that said: the model gets right what matters for deciding. Which constraint
rules, what order of magnitude of hardware it takes, and what each agent added
to the plan costs in users.

## References

The constraints MDI formalizes are each documented in the literature
separately, and more rigorous treatments of the capacity problem do exist
—notably the queueing-theory approaches, which model the arrival of requests
instead of populations. MDI does not compete with them: it adopts a
deliberately operational parameterization (how many users, how much context,
what fraction of the time they generate) because those are the numbers a team
has on hand before buying hardware, and because it makes explicit the trade-off
between populations that aggregate models leave implicit.
